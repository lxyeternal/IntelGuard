Introduction
The 3CXDesktopApp, a popular voice and video conferencing application, has recently become the target of a sophisticated and coordinated supply chain attack. In this blog post, we will delve into the key findings and insights of the incident, providing a comprehensive examination of how the attack unfolded, and implications for users and organizations.
Overview of 3CXDesktopApp
3CXDesktopApp is an enterprise call routing software developed by 3CX, a business communications software company. The application is utilized across multiple platforms, including Windows, macOS, Linux, and mobile devices. With customer organizations in the automotive, food & beverage, hospitality, managed information technology service provider (MSP), and manufacturing sectors, 3CXDesktopApp boasts over 600,000 customer companies and 12 million daily users.
The Significance of Targeting 3CX
Voice and video conferencing applications like 3CXDesktopApp are prime targets for cybercriminals due to their extensive reach and the sensitive nature of the data they handle. By compromising such software, attackers can eavesdrop on an organization’s communications, manipulate call routing, or covertly access voice services.
Timeline Breaking Down
The 3CX supply chain operation is suspected of beginning in the fall of 2022, with several users reporting alerts by mid-March 2023. Initially, these alerts were largely dismissed as false positives. By the end of March, various security vendors, including SentilOne, Cybereason, and CrowdStrike had observed malicious activity on both Windows and macOS systems originating from the 3CXDesktopApp, where CrowdStrike identified the primary malicious binary as a compromised version of ffmpeg.dll. The next day, on March 30, 3CX officially released a security warning, indicating that several of their applications had been compromised with Trojan malware and distributed through the supply chain to their clientele.
Multi-Stage Attack Chain: A Simplified Breakdown
The attack on the 3CXDesktopApp unfolds in a series of stages, beginning with the application acting as a “shellcode loader” — a program responsible for loading and executing malicious code. The shellcode is executed from heap space, an area of memory allocated for dynamic operations. The payload is then retrieved from a specific GitHub repository, after which the attack incorporates infostealer functionality to gather system and browser data.
Stage 1: Trojanized Installer and Altered DLLs
The infection starts with a Trojanized installer (MSI) of the 3CX VOIP software, which sneaked into the release process through a compromised dependency. This installer, signed with the company’s code signing certificate, appeared legitimate. The installer contains modified copies of ffmpeg.dll and d3dcompiler_47.dll. When the 3CX desktop client launches, its dependencies, including ffmpeg.dll, are loaded. The changed DllMain function in ffmpeg.dll loads a harmful portable executable (PE) file inside d3dcompiler_47.dll.
Stage 2: Decoding and Running the Payload
Ffmpeg.dll finds d3dcompiler_47.dll and reads the encrypted shellcode blob into a heap buffer. After decrypting the buffer, it marks the buffer as executable using VirtualProtect and runs the payload. The shellcode loads a DLL reflectively, removing the “MZ” at the start. That DLL is then called with specific arguments and the size of the User-Agent string.
Stage 3: Fetching Icon Files and Decoding Base64 Data
This stage involves downloading icon files from a dedicated GitHub repository. These ICO files have Base64 encoded strings at the end. The data is decoded and used to download another stage. At this point, the DLL seems to be a previously unknown infostealer designed to work with browser data, likely enabling future operations as the attackers analyze the infected customers’ data.
Stage 4: Infostealer Functionality
The final stage uses infostealer functionality, gathering system information and browser data from Chrome, Edge, Brave, and Firefox browsers. This includes checking browsing history, cookies, cached data and images, bookmarks, auto-fill forms, user logins, and data from the Places and History tables for Firefox- and Chrome-based browsers, respectively.
Throughout this multi-stage attack chain, the compromised 3CXDesktopApp serves as the entry point, with each following stage adding more complexity and functionality. The attack ultimately leads to sensitive data exposure and theft, posing significant risks for both individual users and organizations.
Connecting the Attack to LABYRINTH CHOLLIMA
With a high degree of certainty, experts link the attack to the DPRK-affiliated threat actor LABYRINTH CHOLLIMA, a North Korean group. This group is suspected of targeting 3CX’s communication software to infiltrate organizations’ communication systems, potentially manipulating call routing or covertly accessing voice services.
The Aftermath and Recommendations
As a result of the attack, removing the 3CX software from endpoints is recommended until the vendor confirms that future installers and builds are safe. The full extent of the attack and its consequences are unknown, and it remains unclear which organizations were ultimately targeted for the final payload.
Conclusion
The 3CXDesktopApp supply chain attack highlights the growing sophistication of cyber threats and the importance of robust cybersecurity measures to protect against such attacks. Organizations should prioritize implementing robust security strategies, including: cyber threat intelligence tools, high-fidelity detection systems, and continuous monitoring and response capabilities, to minimize the risk of future supply chain attacks and safeguard their valuable information and communication systems.
