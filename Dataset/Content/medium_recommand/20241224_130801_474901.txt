Mr. Secretary-General — OECD, Mr Deputy Minister — Ministry of Science and ICT of the Republic of Korea, Mr President, KISA, Excellencies, Distinguished Delegates, Ladies and Gentlemen.
Good morning.
I would like to thank KISA and the Ministry for being wonderful hosts of this Global Forum, and
I thank the team from the OECD for their kind invitation to give this keynote.
Theme 2, focusing on Collaboration for more secure and resilient supply chains, addresses a growing challenge which can only be overcome through effective international collaboration.
Supply chains are the backbone of public and private organisations.
The use case synonymous with supply chain security is the SolarWinds cyber security incident discovered in December 2020, which involved a sophisticated cyberattack that compromised the software supply chain of SolarWinds, an IT management software provider based in the United States.
In that attack, threat actors inserted a malicious backdoor code into the software update of the SolarWinds’ Orion platform distributed between March and June 2020.
Now, the common advice is that when you are asked to update your software, you should do it as the update will patch the software against the latest discovered vulnerabilities.
But in this case, the corrupted software update introduced more vulnerabilities!
The impact was global, since the platform is widely used by government agencies, Fortune 500 companies, and other organisations.
Although the attackers focused on high-value targets, it was estimated that up to 18,000 customers may have downloaded the malicious update.
Despite the gloom and doom, as an optimist, I saw some digital security wins in this use case:
- The SolarWinds incident was an excellent example of private-public collaboration.
- It prompted cybersecurity reviews and legislative actions in the United States (for example the “White House Executive Order on improving the Nation’s Cybersecurity” (EO 14029). 
There was a ripple effect. Many other countries have also started to strengthen their defences against similar attacks.
- Like the automobile industry advancing its safety standards such as seat belt regulations, or ANCAP safety standards, digital security as a sector is advancing into new maturity phases.
Effectively, SolarWinds challenges us to think deeper:
How do we prevent this from happening again?
Besides assuring organisational trust, we now have to look at the trustability of every single software update. Is this achievable in a dynamic environment?
Solarwinds was the high-profile example which made it to the news.
Concerningly, on a daily basis, organisations are disrupted by digital security threats introduced via trusted third parties.
Many of these organisations are small and medium enterprises (SMEs) — which form over 95% of firms in OECD economies.
Which leads us to think about scale:
Are the current policies scalable, and fit-for-purpose for all organisations including SMEs, and not just the large enterprises?
Currently, across supply chains, suppliers articulate their compliance through certification, or through back-and-forth emails sending very large compliance spreadsheets.
Some companies even have full-time staff working on filling in these spreadsheets!
In the current digital age, is this approach truly the most effective way forward?
What are the levers of change catalysing an effective supply chain resilience uplift?
From my research and observations, I observed 5 important levers.
The first lever is effective legislation. The keyword being effective.
I recall a conversation I had at one of the ISO standards meetings with my friend, Annie Sokol, who was representing the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in that meeting.
I asked her:
“Why are non-American organisations around the world following the NIST guidelines and special publications in cybersecurity, even though they are not based in the US or have no exports to the US?”
She pointed to me:
“Ryan, you have to look at the FISMA act”.
That led me to study the global impact of FISMA, which I cover in my university lectures.
While Federal Information Security Modernization Act (FISMA) sets the legal requirement for annual compliance for all US federal agencies, NIST is responsible for developing the standards and policies that agencies use.
Due to the annual compliance requirements, federal agencies must comply with the guidelines and frameworks written by NIST.
This means these agencies’ IT suppliers, such as MSPs, now also have to help the agencies comply with the NIST guidelines if they want to do business with these agencies.
And what is more interesting is that this spreads across global supply chains to other countries through multi-national companies.
Since these MNCs are also selling other countries, their NIST-compliant platforms are also used by other countries — raising the security maturity across these countries.
Over time, it became a ‘norm’.
Businesses around the world started adopting the NIST guidelines and frameworks as their benchmark.
FISMA rightly identified the upstream, for a global downstream.
The second lever is the ‘ticket to trade’.
Businesses will do their utmost to get the sale.
However, for this to work across the entire supply chain, we need to consider this lever for allbusiness types and sizes.
SMEs are usually neglected in this area– but governments are starting to realise that by uplifting cyber resilience of their SMEs, they are uplifting more than 90% of businesses in their economies.
SME’s and civil society organisations’ limited ability and capacity to manage digital security risk is also recognised in OECD’s Recommendation of the Council on Digital Security Risk Management adopted in 2022.
A 2023 Mastercard study surveying more than 1000 Australian Small Business Leaders and Employees revealed that the top three priorities for small businesses are:
1. Revenue-driving activity such as customer acquisition (62%),
2. Client relationships (60%)
3. Growth (54%)
Where is security??
In this study, only 22% said that Cyber security was a priority, and often, it was cited as their lowest priority.
While over half worry about the risk of attacks, 52% also say it is too costly for them to invest in cyber security.
This is aligned to another report by the Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC) which states that on average, SMEs are only willing to spend $500 (Australian dollars) per year on cyber security.
In other words, unfortunately, the truth is that SMEs do not have cyber security as their top concern. It’s not even their top 3 concerns!
So just increasing awareness may not lead to action.
We have to align to their priorities which incentivise them!
The profitability incentive is also aligned to Adam Smith’s Invisible Hand metaphor in The Wealth of Nations, where he talks about:
The incentives which free markets sometimes create for self-interested people to act unintentionally in the public interest.
The key question now is: How do we make self-interested supply chains act unintentionally to uplift their digital security?
This question led the CSCAU (an Australian industry council I co-founded) to create the SMB1001, a low-cost, dynamic, multi-tier cyber security certification for SMBs.
If the ISO/IEC 27001 certification was the Taekwondo ‘black belt’ of cyber security, the SMB1001 standard’s levels 1 to 5 were designed to be the ‘coloured belts’ before attaining the ISO black belt.
In fact, Level 1 starts with only 6 security controls, and SMBs incrementally progress up the levels to a maximum of 45 controls at Level 5.
With SMB1001, for example, a large supermarket chain can now ask all its 10,000 suppliers to be certified at Level 1 and move to Level 2 the following year.
Our hypothesis was that suppliers to the supermarket chain will be self-interested and certify towards the prescribed levels.
However, the reality is a bit harsher and different.
Standardisation experts here will know the typical adoption challenges of new standards rolled out.
Since certification started in January 2024, uptake was initially slow in the first three months as we initially pitched the standard to the SMEs or the large organisations… that was until we found the key group naturally incentivised to act in their self-interest:
The MSPs, the whipping boys of supply chain security.
MSPs quickly certified themselves, and started to evangelise the SMB1001 standard, as they identified the standard as an independent triage tool to recommend their own services and products to their clients.
In the process, intentionally or unintentionally, they also help their clients uplift their cyber maturity.
At scale, it became an “Invisible Hand”, where as of June 2024, we signed up close to 100 MSPs, and they are now rolling out SMB1001 to their own customers.
Since MSPs have on average 100 clients while a few large ones have thousands of clients, we managed to scale and achieve adoption momentum in half a year.
If things stay constant, SMB1001 is now on track to our 200,000 Australian SMBs certified target by 2026.
This leads us to Lever 3: Create cost savings for organisations
In the same Mastercard report mentioned earlier, 31% of small businesses surveyed have been forced to make cuts to cybersecurity due to rising cost pressures, with 63% claiming they are actively trying to scale down business costs amidst the cost-of-living crisis.
There is a desire by SMEs not to do anything extra. They are resistant to extra burdens or expectations.
This leads us to the next lever, the 4th Lever: “Make security implicit”
A good example for implicit security applications are messaging services like WhatsApp and Signal.
End-to-end security is achieved without day-to-day users knowing the encryption approaches implemented behind-the-scenes.
Often, users do not wish to be bogged down by the details of security implementation.
Put simply, they just want to get on with their jobs.
In some schools of thought, there are arguments that increasing trust results in supply chains being exposed to more risk, not less.
These approaches such as Zero Trust security models advocating for the principle of “never trust, always verify,” rejecting the traditional perimeter-based approach to security in favour of a more granular and adaptive approach.
But is a zero-trust approach in supply chains practical, and can this be implemented by all business types and sizes? These questions will be covered by Session 4 later.
Which brings us to the final Lever: “Make policies and technologies achievable by all.”
From a policy perspective, like the Taekwondo coloured belts example, the reality is that now, most supply chains are serviced by organisations which do not put security at the front of mind.
Just like we do not expect ourselves to speak fluently when we learn a new language, it is unrealistic to expect organisations with no cyber awareness to instantly become a digitally secure organisation.
In Conclusion, with easy wins and achievable steps, catalysed by incentivised stakeholders such as MSPs or IT providers, we could make digital security uplift achievable at scale.
Let us create effective policies and legislations which catalyse an Invisible Hand towards supply chain resilience.
Thank you.

Biography:
Professor Ryan Ko is Chair Professor and Director of UQ Cyber, an interdisciplinary research centre involving 13 schools, faculties and centres at The University of Queensland (UQ), Australia since 2019. For more than a decade, he has served as an expert at ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27, edited two standards in the ISO/IEC 27000 family, co-authored Australia’s SMB1001 certifiable cyber standard for SMEs, and recently co-chaired Singapore’s standards on Cyber Essentials and Cyber Trust Mark. Prior to his role at UQ, he was the founding director of the NZ Institute of Security and Crime Science from 2017 to 2019, established NZ’s first cyber security lab (CROW) in 2012 at the University of Waikato, and was commissioned by NZ’s Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet to draft NZ’s national cyber security curriculum (NZQA Level 6). Through his academic roles, he co-founded several national and global cyber security competitions. His research interests focus on user data control, provenance, privacy, autonomous cyber, cybercrime prevention, and supply chain and organisational cyber resilience, and he has published more than 160 publications including books, patents and articles across computer science, political science, management, and criminology. His research has been cited by policy advisors across Australia, Singapore, and New Zealand, and some have been commercialised globally — helping INTERPOL attribute global cybercrime payments, the US Treasury to attribute fraud within cloud environments, and critical infrastructure providers to monitor industrial cyber threats. An experienced company director and technical expert, he has held governance and advisory roles at INTERPOL, ORCID, listed companies, start-ups, NGOs, and the governments of Australia (ACSC, TEQSA), New Zealand (DPMC, Ministry of Justice, GCSB), Singapore (Enterprise Singapore, IMDA, CSA) and Tonga (MEIDECC). He also contributed to the establishment of CERT NZ and CERT Tonga. Professor Ko has a B.Engineering (Comp. Eng’g) (Hons.), and a Ph.D. from Nanyang Technological University, Singapore. For his contributions to his field, he has been elected Fellow of the Australian Computer Society, the Cloud Security Alliance, and the Queensland Academy of the Arts and Sciences.
