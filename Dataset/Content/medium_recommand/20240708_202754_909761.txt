The open-source ecosystem plays an essential role in today’s software development landscape. It enables developers to collaborate, share, and build upon each other’s work, accelerating innovation and software quality. However, this vast ecosystem also presents unique security challenges. When attackers infiltrate the open-source supply chain by distributing malicious packages, they put countless projects and organizations at risk.
This blog will delve into the following:
1. Why simply reporting on detected malicious packages isn’t enough to protect the community in the open-source ecosystem.
2. How implementing a retro-hunting system can establish comprehensive protection against open-source security threats
The Hidden Dangers of Deleted Malicious Packages
When malicious packages are disclosed to the various package managers, the package managers typically delete the associated release artifacts and metadata. This prevents users from downloading the malware, but it also hinders defenders in two key ways:
1. Lack of documentation: Without a record of the deleted package, it becomes difficult for defenders to understand the scope and impact of the threat.
2. Loss of learning opportunities: By removing the evidence, defenders lose the chance to study the attacker’s methods and tactics, which could help prevent similar attacks in the future.
While some package managers display a notice on the package’s page indicating that a package has been removed, this information primarily benefits users actively searching for these packages. Unfortunately, for those who have already downloaded the packages during their availability, they may remain oblivious to the malicious package lurking within their systems. A case in point is a malicious package that was removed from the Nuget package manager, leaving users who had previously downloaded it potentially unaware of the security risk it posed.
When we began working on malicious packages, we faced numerous instances where we sought data and evidence of past attacks (including recent incidents), but the information was unavailable.
The following are some more examples from various package managers:
NPM
NPM retains a placeholder containing the package’s name after wiping all other data from the registry, including the code, user account, and additional metadata. This is also the case for the REST API.
PyPi
PyPi completely eliminates all traces of the package, leaving no placeholders behind. Although malicious usernames often remain, they appear without any associated malicious packages. Consequently, anyone who encounters these malicious usernames may be unaware of the user’s history of uploading malicious packages.
Real-World Incidents
Let’s examine some past incidents demonstrating the need for better monitoring and sharing of information within the open-source ecosystem.
Incident 1: Malicious Packages Across Languages
In this case, malicious packages across multiple programming languages had gone undetected for years. These packages were all linked to the same attacker and a well-known malicious package that had been known to the community for quite some time. The fact that these packages remained undetected for so long can be attributed, at least in part, to the insufficient information sharing and communication among different language ecosystems. This incident also highlighted the fact that supply chain attacks are not restricted to a specific programming language.
Incident 2: Uncovering the Scope of Lofygang’s Coordinated Attacks
Lofygang, an attack group, operated for over a year with multiple hacking objectives, including credit card information theft, Discord Nitro upgrades, streaming service accounts (e.g., Disney+), and Minecraft accounts. Their activities were only exposed after a malicious package, discovered by the Checkmarx SCS team, led to the investigation and discovery of additional related packages, user accounts, and connections to past supply chain attacks.
Various vendors had reported on some of Lofygang’s malicious packages over the year without realizing they were all part of a larger attack campaign. Our retro-hunting system that collected package-related evidence over time allowed us to piece together the puzzle and reveal the full extent of Lofygang’s operations.
Retro-Hunting Systems: A Proactive Defense Strategy
Retro-hunting systems, like the one created by Checkmarx, are instrumental in offering invaluable insights into the open-source ecosystem. We collect and index all packages and their corresponding metadata from all package managers into Elasticsearch.
Additionally, we store the content of all packages in an extensive data repository to enable in-depth analysis.
This allows us to:
Uncover patterns and connections: By correlating information from packages within the same ecosystem as well as across different programming languages, defenders can reveal relationships between seemingly unrelated incidents, providing a more comprehensive view of potential threats.

Identify indicators of compromise and malware structures: Retro-hunting systems can help detect and block future attacks by identifying indicators of compromise, such as known malicious IP addresses, hashes and domains. Also malware structures (e.g., the recent WASP malware).
Track malicious package authors: Retro-hunting systems can analyze package metadata and historical data, helping defenders identify patterns and connections between packages and authors to gain insights into attackers’ methods and objectives. An example of this can be found in our recent report on the skeleton squad
Broadening the Scope of Retro-Hunting Systems
In addition to the use cases mentioned above, the following are just some of the many other contexts in which our retro-hunting systems can be employed:
Thwarting Typosquatting attempts: By monitoring package names and metadata, retro-hunting systems can detect potential Typosquatting attempts where attackers create malicious packages resembling popular, legitimate ones.
Analyzing the history of package updates: By analyzing package update records, retro-hunting systems can uncover suspicious customs, such as rapid version changes or sudden shifts in code quality.
Conclusion
Retro-hunting systems are a superpower in safeguarding the open-source ecosystem. They equip defenders with the ability to recognize patterns, monitor threats, and gain insight into attackers’ activities. This helps the community stay ahead of the game and ensures the ongoing security of open-source projects. Our own retro-hunting system is a prime example of a powerful tool that uncovers the bigger picture behind incidents and identifies threats in their early stages.
By gathering and preserving data across various package managers, our retro-hunting system enables more in-depth analysis and stronger connections between pieces of information. This allows us to be proactive in detecting, tracking, mitigating threats, and ultimately contributing to a more secure open-source environment.
As the open-source community continues to thrive, leveraging advanced retro-hunting systems like ours will be essential in maintaining the integrity, security, and future success of the open-source ecosystem. By showcasing our retro-hunting system’s capabilities, we demonstrate our commitment to promoting a safer, more resilient open-source world for everyone.
